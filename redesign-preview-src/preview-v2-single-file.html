Memory. Action. Continuity.

AskGogo knows what is on your plate, and does something about it.

It lives in WhatsApp, so there is nothing to install and nothing new to learn. You write to it the way you would write to a capable friend.

Enterprise

  • Shared memory with roles and access controls

Principles

How SecondBrain treats what it knows.

Provenance

Every remembered fact carries its source: you said it, a document showed it, or a calendar confirmed it. Nothing guessed is presented as known.

Correction

You can see what is remembered, change it, mark it private, or delete it. Deleting removes the fact rather than hiding it.

Scope

Personal memory stays personal. Company memory follows the company's permissions. The two never mix by default.

Expiry

A flight ends, a gate code changes, an offer lapses. Facts are allowed to age out instead of being acted on after they stop being true.

Why an assistant should finish the work.

Most AI hands back homework. The useful part is the last mile, and that is where an assistant should spend its effort.

Ask most AI assistants to renew your vehicle insurance and you will get a tidy answer. It will list a few insurers, explain what a no-claim bonus is, and suggest you compare quotes before the policy lapses. All of it is correct, and none of it renews the insurance.

The work is still yours. You still open three websites, type the registration number three times, wait for an OTP, discover that one site wants the previous policy number, find that policy in your email, pay, download the PDF and put it somewhere you will remember. The assistant handled the part that took two minutes. You handled the part that took forty.

Where the time actually goes

When we looked at the tasks people sent to AskGogo, the pattern was consistent. Deciding what to do was rarely the hard part. People knew they needed a service appointment, a refund, a visa slot or a follow-up email. What they lacked was the time and patience for the steps in between: forms, logins, waiting, checking back.

An answer does not remove those steps. It describes them. So we set a different bar: an assistant should be measured by tasks completed, not questions answered.

A good answer describes the work. A good assistant does it, then shows you the receipt.

Finishing takes three things

Completing real tasks is harder than answering questions, and it rests on three capabilities that each have to work.

  • Memory. To book your car service without twenty questions, the assistant needs to know which car, which service centre and that you prefer mornings. Without memory, every task starts with an interview.
  • Autonomy. It has to operate the same websites and inboxes you do: sign in, fill the form, handle the OTP prompt by asking you for the code, and carry on.
  • Judgement about when to stop. Finishing does not mean doing everything alone. Paying, booking and messaging someone else are steps where the assistant should pause and ask, with the details in front of you.

Take away any one of these and you are back to homework. Memory without autonomy produces a well-informed list. Autonomy without memory produces a stream of questions. Either without judgement produces something you cannot trust.

What done means

We define a task as done only when there is evidence the world changed: a booking reference, a calendar entry, a sent email in the thread, a saved receipt. If the assistant cannot observe that result, the task is not done, and it should say so plainly.

That standard is stricter than it sounds. Websites time out. Payment pages fail silently. A form accepts input and then quietly drops it. An assistant that reports success because it clicked the final button will be wrong often enough that you stop relying on it. One that checks the outcome earns the right to be handed the next task.

Why this matters more in India

Much of daily life here runs through portals that were not built for convenience: logins by mobile number and OTP, government appointment systems, utility sites with their own logic. WhatsApp is where people already manage their lives. An assistant that lives there and can handle the portal on your behalf, asking you only for the OTP and the final yes, removes a real and recurring cost.

That is the assistant we are building. It does not try to be impressive in conversation. It tries to leave you with fewer open loops at the end of the day than you had at the start.

Memory is the product.

An assistant that forgets you between conversations is a search box with manners. Persistent memory changes what an assistant can be trusted to do.

Most chat assistants begin every conversation from nothing. You explain that you are vegetarian, that your father's follow-up is in November, that you fly out of Bengaluru, and the next day you explain it again. The model is capable. It simply has no past with you.

That limits more than convenience. Without memory, an assistant cannot act on your behalf, because acting well depends on knowing your context. Booking a table means knowing who is coming and what they eat. Paying a bill means knowing which account it comes from. Reminding you of a deadline is only useful if it knows the deadline exists.

Remembering is easy. Remembering well is not.

Storing everything a person says is trivial. The hard part is deciding what a fact means, how sure we are of it, and when it stops being true. We think about memory in four properties.

  • Provenance. Every fact should carry where it came from. "You told me on 2 September" is different from "a document suggested it", and both differ from a guess. When the assistant uses a fact, it should be able to show its source.
  • Correction. People change their minds and their circumstances. Memory has to be visible and editable, and forgetting has to be real: deleted means gone, not hidden.
  • Scope. What you tell your personal assistant should not appear in a shared workspace, and company knowledge should follow company permissions. Memory needs boundaries as clear as file permissions.
  • Expiry. Many facts have a shelf life. A flight lands, an address changes, an offer ends. A memory system that never lets facts age will eventually act on something stale, with confidence.
The test of memory is not recall. It is whether the assistant uses the right fact at the right moment, without being asked twice.

Memory should be used quietly

The best use of memory rarely announces itself. When you ask for a restaurant on Friday and the options already exclude mushrooms and keep Monday evening free, memory has done its job. When you ask "what was that hotel?" on the web and get the one you sent on WhatsApp on Tuesday night, it has done its job across surfaces.

We also think an assistant should be restrained about what it shows of its memory. Pointing to the source of a fact helps you trust it. Narrating everything it knows about you does not.

Memory as the base layer

This is why memory sits underneath everything in AskGogo, and why we are developing it as its own layer, SecondBrain. Reminders, briefings, documents, travel and browser tasks all read from it and write back to it. A task that finishes leaves the memory slightly better informed, so the next task needs fewer questions.

Over months, that compounding is the difference between a tool you use and an assistant you rely on.

Autonomy you can check.

Agent demos look effortless. Daily use is where they break. Reliable autonomy comes from boring disciplines: planning, boundaries, and checking the result.

An agent that books a flight in a demo video and an agent you would let book your flight are different things. The first needs to succeed once. The second needs to succeed on a slow Tuesday, on a site that changed its layout last week, with your card and your name on the line.

When autonomous agents fail in practice, they tend to fail in a few recognisable ways. They stop halfway and report success. They act confidently on a wrong assumption. They loop on a page they do not understand. Or they do something consequential that nobody approved.

Plan first, then act

Before an agent touches a website, it should know what finished looks like and what the steps are likely to be. A plan makes failures visible: if step three was "choose a slot" and the agent is now on a payment page, something went wrong.

Small operational rules matter here more than clever models. One we learned the hard way: on most shopping and booking sites, sign in first, then add to the cart. Many sites let you build a cart as a guest and then ask you to log in at checkout, which can empty the cart or lose the saved address. Signing in first avoids a whole class of failures.

Draw the boundary before you need it

Some actions are cheap to undo and some are not. Reading a page, filling a form and searching for options can run on their own. Paying, booking, cancelling and sending a message to another person should wait for a yes, with the exact details shown: the amount, the date, the recipient.

Autonomy is not the absence of approvals. It is knowing exactly which steps need one.

Credentials deserve their own boundary. Passwords and card details belong in a vault that the agent can use without the model ever seeing them. When a site asks for a one-time password, the agent should ask you for it in the chat, use it, and move on.

Check the result

The final click is not the end of a task. The end is evidence that the task worked: a confirmation number on the page, a confirmation email in the inbox, an event on the calendar. An agent should look for that evidence before it tells you anything is done.

When it cannot find the evidence, the honest report is "I could not confirm this", along with what it saw. That is less satisfying than "Done!", and far more useful.

Know when to hand over

Some tasks will not complete. A site blocks automated access, a form needs a document you have not shared, an option requires a judgement only you can make. A reliable agent recognises this early and hands over cleanly, with a direct link to the page and a summary of where it stopped, instead of trying the same thing ten times.

None of this is glamorous. It is the difference between an agent that impresses you once and one you hand the next task to without thinking about it.
